A walk through light, texture, and color.
A film-inspired process in Dark Melon.

Explore the process ↓

A film look starts before the tone curve.

These photographs accompany a way of developing: let light spread and fall off, let the exposure shape the texture, then decide how it becomes a finished image.

Dark Melon puts its optical and film effects in that order. The result is a connected process: changing the light changes what comes afterward.

The path through the image
  1. RAW balance + offset
  2. HDF
  3. Vignette
  4. Halation
  5. Grain
  6. Tone + Dye Response
  7. RGB balance
  8. OKLab color
  9. Clarity

01 Before the film

Light first. Texture later.

In a camera, a diffusion filter and the lens change the light before it reaches the film. Dark Melon follows that order: HDF spreads scene-linear light, then Vignette reduces exposure toward the crop’s corners, before halation, grain, or tone mapping.

Those changes feed the later stages. They influence the exposure that drives grain, the light available to create halation, and what ultimately reaches white in the final print. The glow and shading are part of the exposure, rather than overlays placed on a finished photograph.

02 Beyond print white

White is an output. Not the end of the exposure.

A bright lamp and an even brighter reflection may both become plain white in SDR. Halation still needs to know the difference. Dark Melon builds it from the pre-tone linear image and ties its baseline strength to the configured HDR output brightness.

That relationship also applies when you export SDR: keep HDR enabled in the edit, then choose an SDR export. The light feeding the glow retains its gradation even where the final print has run out of room. Turning HDR off in the edit disables halation.

03 Exposure, then grain

A texture that belongs to the exposure.

The grain distribution is evaluated against linear exposure at the grain stage, before the SDR or HDR tone curve. Its tonal weighting can favor shadows or gather around the midtones without being reassigned simply because the final image is made brighter or darker.

Think of the tone curve as the printing or scanning interpretation of an already textured image. The same underlying grain is carried through that interpretation, rather than sprinkled onto the finished print according to its display brightness.

04 Dye Response

Color channels need not respond alike.

Film’s color-forming layers do not have identical exposure responses. Dye Response is a compact approximation of that idea: red, green, and blue can respond with slightly different contrast through the tone-mapping stage.

Only their relative differences count. Moving all three together does not add a second global contrast control. Small offsets can change how colors separate through shadows and highlights—a chemistry-inspired response, rather than a flat color wash.

05 One roll, two white balances

Set the stock. Then finish the photograph.

A roll of film has a fixed color balance; finishing happens afterward. Dark Melon’s two white-balance stages let you work with that distinction. Use RAW temperature and tint as the early, capture-side baseline and keep them consistent across a set for a “one roll of film” look.

RGB white-balance gains act later, after the optical effects, grain, and tone mapping. They are a finishing correction on top of that baseline. The two stages are deliberately different: matching the final neutral does not necessarily produce the same halation, grain response, or highlight behavior.

06 Light between layers

Red travels further.

Color film’s layer structure gives halation its characteristic warm bias. Dark Melon approximates that behavior with a stronger, wider red contribution, a weaker and tighter green contribution, and no added blue halo in color mode.

Transmission controls the green contribution. Its shorter reach can leave a warmer yellow core close to a light and a redder fringe farther away, instead of coloring the entire glow a single uniform hue. It remains an exposure-stage effect, before the final tone curve.
